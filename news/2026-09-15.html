The EPA Repeals the 2024 Power Plant Greenhouse Gas Rule and Proposes to Give Up Its Authority to Regulate Power-Plant Climate Pollution

Al Jazeera · NBC News · UPI · The Washington Times · 2026-09-14

Al Jazeera, NBC News and UPI reported on September 14 that the US Environmental Protection Agency announced it was ending the 2024 greenhouse gas standards for coal- and gas-fired power plants. The rule required coal plants to install carbon capture equipment or shut down by set deadlines. EPA Administrator Lee Zeldin announced the decision alongside Interior Secretary Doug Burgum and Energy Secretary Chris Wright at the G20 energy-abundance meeting in Houston. According to Reuters, as relayed by NBC News, Zeldin said Biden-era actions were designed to "suffocate our economy in order to protect the environment" and intended to regulate the coal industry "out of existence"; he also said "Americans have been demanding more common sense at federal agencies." The EPA said the repeal would save industry more than $300 billion. Al Jazeera reported that the agency issued a supplemental proposal the same day arguing that greenhouse gas emissions from power plants do not cause or contribute to air pollution endangering public health — a finding that, if made final, would end the agency's authority to regulate power-plant climate pollution in future. On figures cited by Al Jazeera, US power plants account for roughly a quarter of the country's climate pollution. Among critics, Maggie Coulter of the Center for Biological Diversity said "denying the existence of a quarter of the country's climate pollution is utterly reckless," and Vickie Patton of the Environmental Defense Fund said "tearing down our national protections against climate pollution will have enormous costs." Two Steps: From Repealing a Rule to Arguing There Is No Rule Left to Write

Two documents on September 14

According to Al Jazeera, NBC News and UPI, the US Environmental Protection Agency did two things on September 14. The first was a final rule repealing the 2024 greenhouse gas standards for coal- and gas-fired power plants, which required coal plants to install carbon capture equipment or shut down by set deadlines. The second was a supplemental proposal arguing that greenhouse gases emitted by power plants do not cause or contribute to air pollution that endangers public health. These are different in kind: the first withdraws a specific rule, the second goes to the legal predicate for writing any power-plant rule under the Clean Air Act. If the second is made final, the agency will no longer hold the authority to regulate power-plant climate pollution in future.

This year's first step came in February

Federal Register and Congressional Research Service documents record that the EPA finalised the rescission of the motor-vehicle portion of the 2009 greenhouse gas endangerment finding in February 2026, and repealed all greenhouse gas emission standards for light-, medium- and heavy-duty on-highway vehicles and engines at the same time; the rule was published in the Federal Register on February 18, 2026. NBC News reported in February that White House press secretary Karoline Leavitt called it "the largest deregulatory action in American history," with the White House saying it would save Americans "$1.3 trillion in crushing regulations." The September 14 step carries the same legal logic from vehicles to power plants.

The figures, by source

The EPA says repealing the 2024 power plant rule will save industry more than $300 billion. A second set of figures cited by Al Jazeera comes from the repealed rule's own estimates and from critics: the rule had been estimated to avoid roughly 30,000 preventable deaths a year and to deliver about $275 billion in annual health and environmental benefits, and US power plants account for roughly a quarter of the country's climate pollution. These sets rest on different methodologies from parties on different sides; this publication labels each by source and does not combine them.

Published notes from the World Resources Institute and the Clean Air Task Force record that the February rescission drew multiple suits from public health, scientific and environmental organisations as soon as it appeared in the Federal Register. Arctic Sea Ice: The March 2026 Maximum Tied the Previous Year's Record Low; the Annual Minimum Usually Falls in Mid-September

NSIDC · NASA · Carbon Brief · Eos · 2026-03 / 2026-09

Analyses published in March by the US National Snow and Ice Data Center and NASA record Arctic sea ice reaching its annual maximum extent of 14.29 million sq km (5.52 million sq miles) on March 15, just below the previous year's 14.31 million sq km (5.53 million sq miles) and statistically tied with it for the lowest maximum in the 48-year satellite record. That figure is 1.36 million sq km (525,000 sq miles) below the 1981-2010 average, roughly twice the area of Texas. Carbon Brief and Eos reported that total Arctic sea ice volume in March 2026 was the lowest on record, about 15 per cent below mid-March 2024. The annual minimum typically falls in mid-September; as this issue goes out, the NSIDC has not published confirmed data for the 2026 minimum. This publication will report the minimum once it is officially confirmed and offers no estimate of its own.
