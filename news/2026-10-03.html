Google's First Project Suncatcher Prototype Reaches Orbit Carrying Four Trillium TPUs on SpaceX's Transporter-18 Mission; the Project's Senior Director Says Contact With the Satellite Is Confirmed

Scientific American · CNBC · Planet Labs press release (via Business Wire) · Startup Fortune · Android Headlines · October 1–2, 2026

Scientific American reported that at approximately 2:32 p.m. EDT on October 1, 2026, a SpaceX Falcon 9 lifted off from Vandenberg Space Force Base in California, placing into low-Earth orbit an experimental satellite carrying Google tensor processing units (TPUs) on the Transporter-18 rideshare mission. According to Planet Labs' press release of October 1, issued via Business Wire, the company launched 20 satellites on the mission: one Project Suncatcher prototype, one Tanager-2 hyperspectral satellite and 18 SuperDoves (Flock 4J). It was Planet's 40th successful launch, bringing the total number of satellites the company has built and delivered to orbit to 718. The release describes the prototype as conducting “the first-ever test of Google's Tensor Processing Units (TPUs) in space”. Startup Fortune and CNBC reported that the Planet-built satellite is about the size of a refrigerator and carries four of Google's Trillium TPUs. Android Headlines quoted Travis Beals, senior director of Project Suncatcher: “Our team has confirmed contact with the satellite, and it is operating as expected.” According to Startup Fortune, the chips run in bursts of about 15 minutes at a time, a limit set by how quickly heat builds up with no air to carry it away; they are powered by roughly 1 kilowatt of solar panels and cooled by vacuum alone. Startup Fortune and TechMyMoney recorded that before launch Google exposed Trillium chips to a proton beam at the University of California, Davis, with no hard chip failure through a total ionizing dose of 15 krad(Si), against roughly 750 rad(Si) anticipated for a shielded mission; Scientific American reported Google's statement that the chips held up “remarkably well” in simulated radiation testing, with data suggesting they could handle more ionizing radiation than they would be expected to encounter over five years in space. Startup Fortune reported that engineers will run a version of Google's open-weight Gemma model on the orbiting processors over the coming year while monitoring radiation effects and thermal stress. Scientific American and TechMyMoney recorded that Google plans to launch two further experimental satellites in 2027 to test high-bandwidth laser communication between prospective orbital data centres.
