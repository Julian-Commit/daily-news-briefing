Swiss Glaciers Lose a Further 5.5% of Their Ice in 2026: Mean Mass Balance of −2.9m Water Equivalent, With Aletsch and Rhône Setting Melt Records

GLAMOS annual report · GLAMOS/SCNAT press release · NPR (via KPBS) · The Associated Press (via Express & Star) · September 30 – October 1, 2026

According to GLAMOS’s report “Annual mass balance of Swiss glaciers in 2025/2026”, annual ice loss was measured on 23 glaciers across the Swiss Alps in September 2026, giving an average mass balance of −2.9 metres water equivalent; extrapolated to all glaciers, this corresponds to a further 5.5% reduction in Swiss ice volume. The report states that after a winter with very little snow, especially in the eastern and southern Alps, long-lasting and intense heatwaves between May and September pushed the mass balance to the second-lowest value on record, after 2022. According to the October 1 press release from GLAMOS and the Swiss Academy of Sciences (SCNAT) cryosphere commission, melt rates from 2022 to 2026 were more than twice as high as in any previously monitored five-year period, amounting to an ice volume loss of almost 20% since 2021; the Aletsch, Rhône, Clariden and Allalin glaciers all recorded their greatest melt on record in 2026, and the mean thickness of individual glaciers fell by between 2.5 and 4 metres. NPR, carried by KPBS on September 30, reported that the 5.5% figure for 2026 is slightly below the 6% recorded in 2022 because winter snow cover was somewhat greater, and recorded that 1,200 smaller glaciers have disappeared over the past 50 years. Matthias Huss, who leads GLAMOS, told NPR: “The warming that we are experiencing now can be attributed 100% to anthropogenic climate change. It’s as simple as that.” In their press release, GLAMOS and SCNAT said that “Alpine glaciers have no chance of surviving in the long term under such conditions”. The report is credited to Huss, with contributions from Andreas Bauder, Andreas Linsbauer and Martina Barandun.

[Method and scope] According to the GLAMOS annual report, Glacier Monitoring Switzerland completed annual ice-loss measurements on 23 glaciers across the Swiss Alps in September 2026. The report states that what it publishes is “a preliminary data evaluation with respect to other years”; the national ice-volume figure is obtained by extrapolating the measured values from those 23 glaciers to all glaciers. The report is credited to Matthias Huss, with contributions from Andreas Bauder, Andreas Linsbauer and Martina Barandun.

[The 2026 figures] The report gives an average mass balance of −2.9 metres water equivalent for the 2025/2026 hydrological year, corresponding on extrapolation to a further 5.5% reduction in Swiss ice volume. The GLAMOS/SCNAT press release of October 1 records that the mean thickness of individual glaciers fell by between 2.5 and 4 metres. NPR, carried by KPBS on September 30, reported that the 5.5% figure for 2026 is slightly below the 6% recorded in 2022, the difference being somewhat greater winter snow cover.

[Breaking the causes down by season] According to the GLAMOS report, this year’s outcome is the sum of two phases: first a winter with very little snow, particularly in the eastern and southern Alps, then long-lasting and intense heatwaves between May and September. Together they pushed the mass balance to the second-lowest value on record, after 2022.

[The five-year picture and individual records] According to the report and the October 1 press release, melt rates from 2022 to 2026 were more than twice as high as in any previously monitored five-year period, and Swiss ice volume has fallen by almost 20% since 2021. The same release records that the Aletsch, Rhône, Clariden and Allalin glaciers all saw their greatest melt on record in 2026; according to the report carried by NPR, Aletsch and Rhône are the two largest glaciers in Switzerland.

[A longer time scale] NPR, via KPBS, reported that 1,200 smaller glaciers in Switzerland have disappeared over the past 50 years, and recorded Huss describing areas that have already melted out: “They are completely devoid of ice. It’s a moon landscape that is dry and dead.” On attribution, Huss told NPR: “The warming that we are experiencing now can be attributed 100% to anthropogenic climate change. It’s as simple as that.”

[Institutional statements] In their press release, GLAMOS and SCNAT said that “Alpine glaciers have no chance of surviving in the long term under such conditions”. According to the report carried by NPR, researchers estimate that if current warming continues all Swiss glaciers will be gone by 2100.
